04+

The connected CFO: deep in finance, wide enough to connect the dots

The next-generation CFO is a generalist with a deep finance core. A war in the Gulf or a warm Pacific reaches the cost of capital in a few months. To see it coming, the CFO needs enough geography, history, technology, law, business, operations and political science to connect the dots, and enough finance to turn them into a decision.

Guardrails: where connecting dots goes wrong

  1. Breadth without depth is commentary. The CFO is accountable for the numbers. Finance must stay deep.
  2. No one is expert in ten fields. The skill is knowing which question to ask, and who to ask.
  3. Seeing patterns that are not there. A good story is not evidence. Test the chain against data at every step.
  4. Remembering only the right calls. We recall the generalists who saw it coming, not the ones who cried wolf.

Turn any world event into a decision: four questions

  1. Where does it hit? Which line of the P&L, balance sheet or cash flow.
  2. How big? A range in money, not a word like "significant".
  3. How likely, and how soon? Odds and timing, written down.
  4. What do I do now, and what would change my mind? One action and one trigger to review it.
TakeawayBreadth helps you see the shock coming. Finance depth turns it into a number. Discipline turns the number into an action.

02

Cash is the constraint; cost of capital is the price of loosening it

A mid-size firm can almost always raise money when yields are low and investors are confident. When yields spike, money costs more, and for weaker borrowers it may not be available at all. So the regime sets both the price and the availability of cash.

    04

    Cost of equity also starts from the yield, then adds risk

    CAPM: cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium. The risk-free rate is the government bond yield. Beta and the risk premium come from market prices and volatility. In a stress period, all three can move at once.

    Stress applies three changes: risk-free rate +1.29 points (the actual US 10-year rise from 27 Feb to 8 Oct 2026), equity risk premium +1.0 point and beta pulled halfway towards 1.0. The last two are illustrative, not measured.

    06

    Two decisions, one funding problem, no hindsight

    You are the CFO. Decide using only what you could see on each date. Then see how your choices play out on two paths: what actually happened, and what could have happened.

    The company

    • Revenue: $500 million. Rated BBB, investment grade.
    • A $150 million bond matures in December 2026. It must be refinanced.
    • Cash on hand: $40 million. The board wants at least $25 million kept as a safety buffer.
    • The oil shock will tie up an extra $20 million in working capital.
    • The board has proposed a $30 million share buyback.

    Your three choices for the $150 million

    Lock now. Issue a 10-year fixed bond today. You get the cash now, at today's rate, but pay interest on both bonds until December.
    Hedge. Fix today's Treasury rate without borrowing yet. If rates rise, the hedge pays you; if they fall, you pay. You still borrow in December, and your credit spread is not fixed.
    Wait. Refinance in December at whatever rate and spread the market offers then.

    Assumptions are illustrative: current credit spread 1.50 points over Treasuries; cash earns the 3-month bill rate.

    TakeawayThe best choice is not the one that wins on the path that happened. It is the one you could live with on every path.
    07

    Five things to keep

    The final session in one page.

    1. Cash is the constraint. Running out of it ends the firm. Everything else is secondary.
    2. Cost of capital is the price of loosening that constraint. It rises for debt and equity at the same time.
    3. Yields drive the cost of capital. Real return, inflation, liquidity, term and default premiums. Know which one is moving.
    4. US Treasury yields are the anchor. They set the direction of global capital flows and the risk-free rate in every valuation.
    5. Read the regime, then act early. Lock in cheap money in good times. Protect liquidity when the signals turn.

    A CFO does not maximise growth. A CFO maximises optionality under constraints.
